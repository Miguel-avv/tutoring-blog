(ii) briefly describe how can self ligation occur and how it can be avoided:
Self-ligation occurs when the digested ends of the vector simply re-anneal and 
ligate back together without an insert being incorporated. This can happen if:
•	The molar ratio of insert to vector is too low, so not enough insert molecules
are available to compete with vector ends, or
•	The vector ends are compatible and can readily re-join in the presence of ligase.

To avoid self-ligation, vectors are often treated with phosphatase, which removes the 
5′ phosphate groups from the vector ends. Without these phosphates, the vector cannot ligate to itself, 
but it can still ligate to an insert that provides intact phosphate groups.

**


(iii)Why self-ligation of vector, but not insert fragments...
If a self-ligated vector is transformed into bacteria, the vector will still carry the 
selectable marker (such as antibiotic resistance). This means colonies can grow on the selection
plate even if the desired insert was not incorporated, making it difficult to distinguish between
colonies with the correct recombinant plasmid and empty self-ligated plasmids.

In contrast, self-ligated insert fragments are not a problem because they do not contain the plasmid’s 
origin of replication or antibiotic resistance gene. They cannot replicate independently and will not
produce colonies on selective media.

**

iV) what happens when bolth the vector and the insert are treated with phosphate:

If both the vector and the insert are treated with phosphatase, ligation cannot occur. 
This is because T4 DNA ligase requires a 5′ phosphate group to join DNA fragments.
If phosphatase removes these groups from both the vector and insert, no phosphodiester bond can form, preventing
the vector and insert from being ligated together.

   •	Type of tag: 6x His-tag, GST-tag, FLAG-tag, etc.

The heat shock method is a common laboratory technique used to introduce foreign DNA, usually in the 
form of a plasmid, into chemically competent bacterial cells. It involves treating the bacteria with a cold calcium 
chloride solution to increase their permeability, followed by a sudden, brief exposure to a high temperature (a "heat shock") which creates 
transient pores in the cell membrane. This process allows the plasmid DNA to enter the bacteria, enabling genetic modification for applications like gene expression and protein production.  
Steps of the Heat Shock Method
Preparation of Competent Cells: Bacteria are treated to become "chemically competent," often using a calcium chloride solution that helps 
neutralize the repulsive negative charges between the DNA and the cell membrane, promoting their interaction.
Incubation on Ice: The chemically competent cells are kept on ice, then mixed with the DNA containing the gene of interest. This cold incubation 
allows the DNA and cells to associate closely, with the calcium ions facilitating this contact
